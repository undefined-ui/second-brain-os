# Zettelkasten, PARA, evergreen notes

None of this is new. The methods people abandoned were mostly right, and it is
worth knowing which parts of them still apply, because the good ideas came with a
cost that has now changed.

## Zettelkasten

Niklas Luhmann's slip-box, popularised for a modern audience by Sönke Ahrens in
"How to Take Smart Notes". Atomic notes, one idea each, densely cross-referenced,
written in your own words.

**Still right:** atomic notes, dense linking, rewriting in your own words rather
than clipping quotes. All three are in this guide's [structuring
rules](../04-structuring/README.md).

**The cost:** every link was manual. Reading one paper meant deciding where it
connected among hundreds of existing notes, which is real cognitive work and the
reason most slip-boxes die in month three.

## PARA

Tiago Forte's system from "Building a Second Brain": Projects, Areas, Resources,
Archive. Organise by actionability rather than by subject.

**Still right:** the insight that project material and reference material want
different structures. That is exactly [the two layers](two-layers.md), arrived at
from a different direction.

**The cost:** constant filing decisions, and the fact that actionability changes,
so the same note moves between categories over time.

## Evergreen notes

Andy Matuschak's approach: notes written to be reread and improved over years,
concept-oriented, densely linked, developed slowly.

**Still right:** the standard for what a good concept page is. Matuschak's
argument that notes should be written for your future self, not as a record of
having read something, is the bar this guide's concept pages aim at.

**The counterweight:** his position is that the writing is the thinking, and
delegating it means not doing the thinking. That is a real objection to this
whole approach and worth sitting with rather than dismissing.

## What changed

One thing: the maintenance is now delegable.

Every system above works. Every one of them failed for the same reason, which is
that they required a human to do boring linking work forever. An agent doing it
does not get tired in month three.

## What did not change

You still have to choose what to read. You still have to decide what matters.
The agent files and connects; it does not have taste, and a vault fed
indiscriminately is a well-organised pile.

And Matuschak's objection stands: understanding comes from writing. The way to
keep it is to write the concept pages that matter to you yourself, and let the
agent handle sources, entities and the linking. That split is a preference this
guide does not enforce, and it is worth deciding deliberately rather than by
default.
