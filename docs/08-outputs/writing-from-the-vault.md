# Writing from the vault

Writing is where the vault stops being an input and starts being leverage. The
research is already done, cited, and connected. What is left is the argument.

## Outline from concept pages, not from a prompt

```
I want to write about why context length does not replace retrieval. Which of my
concept pages are relevant, what do they collectively argue, and where do my
sources disagree?
```

That gives you an outline grounded in things you read, with the weak points
marked. Asking a model to outline the topic from scratch gives you the consensus
view of the internet, which is what everyone else is also writing.

The disagreements are the most useful part. They are where you have something to
say that is not the consensus.

## Cite your own sources

Every claim in a draft traces back through a concept page to a source page to a
URL. That chain is what makes fact-checking a five-minute job instead of an
afternoon.

It also protects you from the specific failure of writing with a model: fluent
sentences containing invented specifics. If a number in the draft has no source
page behind it, it did not come from your research.

## Keep your voice

Drafting from the vault produces the model's phrasing over your material. That is
fine as a first pass and bad as a final one.

The practical split: let the agent assemble structure and evidence, write the
sentences yourself. Or draft with it and rewrite every paragraph. What does not
work is publishing the assembly, which reads exactly like what it is.

## Close the loop

Ingest your own finished work back into the vault.

Your published pieces are sources like any other: they contain your positions,
stated clearly, at a date. Six months later the vault can tell you what you
argued and whether you still believe it.

This is the step almost everyone skips, and it is what makes the second piece in
a domain meaningfully faster than the first.
